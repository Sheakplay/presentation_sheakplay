Definition

What is Audio?

Audio is a form of information that is represented and transmitted through sound.

SpeechMusic PodcastsInterviews Sound effectsAudiobooks

Audio lets people pass on information without written text or images — we simply listen.

The microphone is the most common way to turn sound into a signal we can store.

Physics

How do we hear sound?

Sound is created when an object vibrates. It is a physical wave — and it needs a medium, usually air, to travel.

  • An object vibrates and pushes the air around it.
  • The vibration spreads through the air as a sound wave.
  • The wave reaches our ear and makes the eardrum vibrate.
  • The brain processes those vibrations and recognises them as sound.
The audio chain

Recording & storing audio

Audio can be recorded, stored, edited, transmitted and played back.

  • A microphone captures the sound and converts the waves into an electrical signal.
  • Modern devices convert that signal into digital data.
  • The data is saved as an audio file — ready to edit, copy and send.

Part 2

History of Audio

For most of human history, sound existed only while it was happening. People could speak, sing and play — but there was no way to keep the sound and hear it again.

Recording technology began in the 19th century, when inventors tried to make sound visible and permanent. It moves through four great eras:

Acoustic1857 – 1925 · sound recorded purely mechanically
Electrical1925 – 1940s · microphones and amplifiers
Magnetic1930s – 1980s · tape, editing, stereo
Digital1970s → today · numbers, computers, internet
Device 1 of 7

The Phonautograph · 1857

Who

Édouard-Léon Scott de Martinville, a French editor and typographer, patented it on 25 March 1857. He wanted a machine that could imitate the ear.

How it worked

A horn collected the sound → a thin membrane vibrated → a stylus traced the vibrations onto a surface covered with soot. The result was a picture of the sound — a phonautogram.

The limitation

It could record sound but not play it back. Scott wanted speech to "write itself", but he had no way to turn the lines into sound again.

2008 — the voices return

Researchers scanned old phonautograms and used computers to turn the traces into digital audio — 19th-century sounds became audible again.

The first machine to record sound — it drew the wave instead of playing it.

Device 2 of 7

The Phonograph · 1877

The breakthrough

Twenty years later Thomas Edison built a machine that could record sound and play it back — the first time a voice could be heard again.

Recording

A metal cylinder covered with tin foil rotated. Speech moved a diaphragm and a stylus, and the stylus cut indentations into the soft foil.

Playback

The stylus followed the same groove again, the diaphragm vibrated once more, and a horn made the sound audible.

The limitation

Later versions used wax cylinders — better sound, but making thousands of copies of a cylinder was difficult. That is why discs eventually won.

Edison's tinfoil phonograph, 1877 — the first machine to make sound play back.

Device 3 of 7

The Gramophone & Disc Records · 1887

Who

Emile Berliner patented the gramophone in 1887. Instead of Edison's cylinder, his system used a flat disc.

Why discs won

The recording was cut as a zig-zag groove in the surface. A disc is easy to reproduce in large quantities — perfect for selling music to the public.

From shellac to vinyl

By the early 20th century discs dominated recorded music. Shellac records were replaced by vinyl, and new speeds followed: 78 rpm → 45 rpm singles → 33⅓ rpm LPs.

Berliner's own disc from 1897 — the zig-zag groove cut into a flat, mass-produced format.

Device 4 of 7

Electrical Recording · 1920s

Before 1925

Recording was purely mechanical. Performers had to play straight into a large recording horn, which passed the vibrations to the recording medium.

1925

Microphones, electronic amplifiers and better equipment became standard. A much wider range of sounds could be captured, and volume and balance were controlled electronically.

What changed

Recordings became far more realistic. Quiet instruments and soft voices could be recorded effectively — and audio became central to radio and cinema.

A recording session, 1920s — performers still facing the horn, before microphones took over.

Device 5 of 7

Magnetic Tape · 1930s–1950s

Origins

Magnetic recording had already been shown in the late 19th century, but tape became important in the 20th. Developed in Germany in the 1930s, it became known worldwide after the Second World War.

What was better

Much better sound quality and longer recordings than earlier methods — and, crucially, it could be edited: tape could be cut and joined.

By the 1950s

Tape was the standard of radio and music production. It is what made stereo and multi-track recording possible, and it led to the compact cassette for home use.

The AEG Magnetophon, 1939 — the machine that made tape recording practical.

Device 5 of 7 · continued

What Tape Made Possible

Stereo

Two channels captured the same performance from slightly different positions, giving a much wider, more realistic sense of space.

Multi-track

Musicians recorded different parts separately — drums, then bass, then vocals — and engineers combined them later. Modern music production was born.

The cassette

Tape led to the compact cassette: small, cheap and reusable. For the first time ordinary people could record and listen at home.

Device 6 of 7

The Digital Era · 1970s →

The change

Sound stopped being one continuous physical signal. It became a series of numerical samples that computers can store, process, copy and edit.

1979

The first popular music album recorded entirely digitally was released — and digital recording moved quickly into professional studios and home systems.

Why it matters

A file can be copied without losing quality, edited non-destructively and sent anywhere in the world. Audio becomes data.

Digital audio on screen — the sound is now a waveform you can cut, move and process.

Device 6 of 7 · continued

The Compact Disc · 1980s

Why the CD won

CDs were smaller and far more durable than vinyl records — no scratches from a stylus, no warping, and they could be handled and stacked freely.

Capacity and quality

A single disc could store a large amount of high-quality digital audio with no loss of quality when copied.

Impact

CDs became one of the most popular ways to listen to recorded music and defined the sound of the 1980s and 1990s.

The CD player — a laser reads the digital pits on the disc, with no needle touching the surface.

Device 7 of 7

MP3, Computers & the Internet

Audio becomes a file

Formats such as WAV and MP3 let audio exist as an ordinary computer file, instead of being tied to a physical recording medium.

Compression

MP3 mattered because compression makes files much smaller. You could store huge music collections and send them across the internet.

No physical limits

Digital audio became easy to copy and edit — with none of the wear, noise or effort of discs and tapes.

Sound without a medium — a music collection is now just an ordinary folder on a computer.

Today

Streaming & Audio Today

A change in habits

Instead of buying a physical recording, people download files or listen online.

Since the late 2000s

Streaming has become the dominant way to listen — music, podcasts and audiobooks play through the internet without owning the file.

Right now

Audio can be recorded with something as simple as a smartphone, edited with computer software, and heard around the world in seconds.

Technology turned sound from something temporary into something that can be recorded, edited, stored and shared globally.

Record anywhere — a phone and a laptop are a complete audio studio.

Summary

Key Takeaways

  • 1857 — the phonautograph captured sound, but could not play it back.
  • 1877 — Edison's phonograph made recorded sound playable.
  • 1887 — Berliner's disc made music something you could sell in millions.
  • 1925 — electricity made recording realistic.
  • 1930s — magnetic tape made recording editable, stereo and multi-track.
  • 1970s–80s — numbers, computers and the CD brought perfect copies.
  • 2000s — MP3, the internet and streaming made audio portable and infinite.
  • Today — sound goes from temporary to permanent, editable and global.
